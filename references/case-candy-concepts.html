Confectionery Creative Concept Testing

It didn’t need to get more extreme. It needed to get more relatable.

Five commercial concepts for a sour-candy launch, one assumption in the room that the loudest reaction wins, and a read that said otherwise.

Client
National Candy Brand
Industry

Priority direction + creative recommendation
The instinct in the room

Go louder. It’s sour candy for a young audience — the more extreme the reaction, the better the ad.

That’s a reasonable instinct, and a testable one. The typical version of this test rates five storyboards on how much attention they grab, picks the loudest, and sends it to production. That approach optimizes for spectacle.

We ran seven focus groups instead of a survey, and watched where attention actually went — where it drifted, and where people saw themselves — rather than just asking which ad was the flashiest. What still felt like the brand once the joke landed mattered more than what people remembered a minute later.

The question was never which concept was the most outrageous. It was which one people could see themselves in.

How every concept was judged

One consistent lens, testing the same three things across seven conversations.

Seven focus groups, eighteen participants, recruited to reflect national demographic proportions across age, gender, and ethnicity. All screened as category buyers and non-rejectors of the brand; most had purchased the brand itself in the last six months.

Every room found its own way into the conversation — reactions, tangents, whatever surfaced. What didn’t change was what we measured against: the same three things, concept after concept. That consistency is what makes the comparison real — a diagnosis, not a popularity contest.

1 Rated strong on every dimension measured — one specific visual choice split the room and pulled the overall score below the group it otherwise matches. Execution, not story.  2 Average rating out of 5, shown for reference; it does not sort this grid.

Sorted by brand fit, not by overall score — the two don’t agree, and that disagreement is the finding. Two concepts read as strong across every dimension we measured; two read as weak across every dimension. Both of the strong concepts were grounded, ordinary-life scenarios exaggerated for the joke. Both of the weak ones were abstract, atmospheric, and — memorable as they were — harder to place back on the brand once the visual wore off.

The recommendation

Stop treating the brand’s own device like decoration. Build the next round of concepts around it.

The outcome
5 → 1

Built on the brand’s own signature asset — grounded in real situations, exaggerated one step — not a newer, more abstract direction.

Was

Five concepts on the table, several of them pulling further from the brand’s own visual identity, not closer to it.

Now

One direction, and a standing instruction: build every future concept on the brand’s classic asset and real situations — treat it as the engine, not the decoration.

Your turn

Betting on the loudest concept? Test what people actually see themselves in.

You don’t need five storyboards and a launch deadline to start. One clear read of which one feels true is enough.
