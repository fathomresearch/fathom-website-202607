The setup

Eighteen messages. Eleven opinions. And a deadline.

{{SETUP_LEAD}} — the campaign arrived with eighteen candidate lines and a room in which everyone had a favourite. The usual tie-breaker in that room is seniority.

Rating each line out of ten does not settle it. Everything comes back liked, the gaps between them are inside the margin of error, and the winner turns out to be whichever line was read first. What separates messages is being made to choose — and being made to name the one you would throw out.

Which is the part most message testing skips. The useful thing to know about a claim is rarely how many people love it. It is how many people it loses.

Before anything was tested

You cannot test an idea and a piece of copywriting at the same time.

As submitted

Our new XR-9 formula lasts up to 3× longer than the leading brands* — and it’s now available in 12 new scents!

As tested

Lasts three times longer than the leading brand.

  • Brand and product names removed. Left in, the test measures brand equity and reports it as a claim result.
  • Cut to a single idea — a line carrying two claims cannot tell you which of them people answered.
  • Length matched to every other claim in the set, within two words. Longer lines win on presence alone.
  • Superlatives, asterisks and exclamation marks stripped from all eighteen, or from none of them.
  • Register levelled: same tense, same person, same reading age, so tone is not silently on trial.

All eighteen went through this before any of them met a respondent. It is the least glamorous half of message testing and the half that decides whether the results mean anything — skip it and the winning line is simply the one that happened to be written best.

Then the design does the rest. Each respondent sees six sets of four claims and names the best and the worst in each. Across the sample every claim appears equally often, and against every other claim about equally often. Nobody is shown all eighteen at once: a list of eighteen is a memory test, and the answer is whatever sat at the top.

Ranked on how often it was picked best, the second line looks like a contender. Ranked on how often it was picked worst, it is the riskiest thing in the set — a third of people actively rejected it, and a claim that divides a room in research divides a market in the wild. The line that won did not win on enthusiasm. It won by being the only one almost nobody wanted to throw away.
