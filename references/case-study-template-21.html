The stalled average

Six quarters flat. A loyalty app the company had marketed hard the whole time. The dashboard said nothing was working.

Membership growth is supposed to be the easy metric: get people onto the app, and repeat purchases follow. Here they hadn’t — the network’s 90-day member repeat-purchase rate hadn’t moved in a year and a half, across roughly 1,800 stores, despite continuous promotion of the app.

The average told a simple, discouraging story: the program had hit a ceiling. Read that way, there are only two moves left — spend harder on the app, or accept the ceiling. Both treat the average as the finding.

But an average of 1,800 stores isn’t one number. It’s 1,800 numbers standing in a line, and the average’s whole job is to erase whichever ones are doing something different. So we stopped asking what the average was, and asked what the distribution should look like — then went looking for whoever broke it.

How we verified it

An outlier is either a mistake or the most important row in the data. You don’t get to assume which.

We audited 184 stores — a representative sample of the network — on one metric: the 90-day repeat-purchase rate of enrolled members. Rather than rank stores against the average, we modeled the distribution they should follow, and flagged whoever broke it. One store did, by a wide margin. The instinct at that point is to delete it — a point that far out reads like a data error, and cleaning it up feels like good practice. We do the opposite: a verified outlier is the most information-dense point in the set. But it has to earn that status first.

Every bar is a band of stores by their 90-day repeat-purchase rate — a tight, ordinary bell curve centered on the network mean of 45.3%, standard deviation 3.4 points. Store 114 doesn’t sit at the edge of that curve. It sits 25.9 points past it. A normal distribution does not produce that gap by chance — which is exactly what made it worth verifying, and then worth a plane ticket.

What the field found

“I don’t have a loyalty app that works. I have a notebook.”

— the store manager, on a paper “regulars book” of roughly 230 named customers

She had trained her baristas to greet regulars by name on sight and start their usual order before they reached the counter. Customers noticed. They start making my drink the second I walk in. I’m not going anywhere else.
The outcome
+13 pts

90-day repeat rate, 12 pilot stores, one quarter — the app’s own reorder feature moved the same metric +2 pts in a full year.

Was

45% network repeat rate, unchanged for six quarters.

Now

58% in the 12 stores running the codified “name-and-usual” protocol — now rolling out to all ~1,800.

The single data point three analysts had flagged “exclude — likely error” became the growth program.
