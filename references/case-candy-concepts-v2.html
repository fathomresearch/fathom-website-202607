Confectionery Creative Concept Testing

It didn’t need to get more extreme. It needed to get more relatable.

Five commercial concepts for a sour-candy launch, one assumption in the room that the loudest reaction wins, and a read that said otherwise.

Client
National Candy Brand
Industry
Confectionery
Engagement
Qualitative concept testing
Output
Priority direction + quant plan
The instinct in the room

Go louder. It’s sour candy for a young audience — the more extreme the reaction, the better the ad.

That’s a reasonable instinct, and a testable one. The typical version of this test rates five storyboards on how much attention they grab, picks the loudest, and sends it to production. That approach optimizes for spectacle.

We ran seven focus groups instead of a survey, and watched for something closer to what actually happens once an ad airs: not which one people remember, but which one still feels like the brand once the joke lands — and whether they still want the candy once it’s over.

The question was never which concept was the most outrageous. It was which one people could see themselves in.

How we ran the room

Eighteen people, five concepts, the same four questions every time.

The lens
  1. 01Message clarityDoes the claim land, without tipping into “too much”?
  2. 02Emotional connectDoes the humor actually land, or just sit there?
  3. 03Brand fitDoes it still feel like the brand, or like a different one wearing its packaging?
  4. 04Creative deviceDoes the brand’s own signature bit help sell the joke, or just repeat it?

Every concept got the same four questions, asked the same way, across every group, in a room screened for people who already buy the category — not strangers to it. The scores that came out the other side aren’t a popularity contest. They’re a diagnosis of what’s actually landing, concept by concept.

Where the story landed

The concepts that felt true to people’s own lives read as on-brand. The ones that didn’t, didn’t.

Sorted by brand fit, not by overall score — the two don’t agree, and that disagreement is the finding. Two concepts read as strong across every dimension we measured; two read as weak across every dimension. Both of the strong concepts were grounded, ordinary-life scenarios exaggerated for the joke. Both of the weak ones were abstract, atmospheric, and — memorable as they were — harder to place back on the brand once the visual wore off.

The reframe

The brand didn’t need to get more extreme to sell candy. It needed to get more relatable — then exaggerate the real.

The outcome
5 → 1

Creative concepts narrowed to one direction — built on the brand’s own asset, not a new one — and funded for quantitative validation before production.

Was

Five concepts in the room, and no agreement on which one was safe to scale.

Now

One direction, chosen for fit rather than spectacle, moving into quantitative testing at a larger sample before creative production.

Your turn

Betting on the loudest concept? Test what people actually see themselves in.

You don’t need five storyboards and a launch deadline to start. One clear read of which one feels true is enough.
