The setup

They kept making the product better. People kept not switching to it.

{{SETUP_LEAD}} — on every feature comparison the product won, and the roadmap was a plan to keep winning them. Yet the switching rate would not move, and no amount of “which features do you value” explained why.

Because people do not buy features. They hire a product to make progress in a situation. The comparison a customer runs is never your product against a rival’s; it is the effort of changing against the pain of staying. Win every feature and you can still lose that comparison, because the thing standing between them and you was never a missing feature.

So we stopped asking what people wanted and started reconstructing what people did — the last time they actually switched something, and everything that nearly stopped them.

How the job was found

A purchase is a moment. The decision behind it took fourteen weeks.

So we don’t ask people what they want. We sit them back inside the decision they already made and walk it backwards — from the final click to the first flicker of doubt.

The day itself

The switch happened on an ordinary afternoon, between two meetings. By then it was barely a decision at all — just the last click of one already made.

Surfaced by “Take me to the day you switched. What happened that morning?”

Fourteen weeks earlier

The first thought had arrived months before, as a background irritation no one would have logged as a buying signal — a quiet, recurring sense that the old way cost more than it saved.

Surfaced by “Go back further. When did you first think something had to change?”

The weeks in between

Then came the work-arounds — a spreadsheet, a second app, a favour from a colleague. Each held for a while. The real bar to switching was every one of those small fixes failing, one after another.

Surfaced by “What did you try in between that didn’t stick?”

And even then —

With the decision already made, one fear nearly undid it: moving everything across, finding it worse, and having no way back. The switch almost died after it had been won.

Surfaced by “Even once you’d decided — what almost stopped you?”

The force that decided it — and the one we return to next

We interview the switched, in both directions. People who just moved to you, and people who just moved away — the leavers explain more than the loyal ever will, because they ran the comparison and you lost it. Recency is everything; a switch six months old is already a story someone has tidied up.

And we separate the three jobs inside every decision. The functional job (what it does), the emotional job (how it makes them feel), and the social job (how it makes them look). The functional one is the easiest to ask about and almost never the one that decides.

The largest force in the room
Net Progress 127 · inertia 132. Inertia wins by five — so the switch doesn’t happen on its own.

For two years the brand had been pushing on the pull — more features, better comparisons, a stronger case for arriving. The pull was never the problem. The single biggest arrow on the board is anxiety, and you cannot out-feature an anxiety. Every point added to the pull was landing against a bigger force nobody was touching — the fear of the move itself.
